# Related network-coalescent identifiability results

This is a bounded, manuscript-specific comparison, not a complete priority search or an independent audit of these papers. It distinguishes their observations, model and conclusions from the selected fixed-family two-site result.

## Yu, Degnan and Nakhleh (2012): sampling and topology probabilities

Yun Yu, James H. Degnan and Luay Nakhleh, [“The Probability of a Gene Tree Topology within a Phylogenetic Network with Applications to Hybridization Detection”](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371/journal.pgen.1002660), *PLOS Genetics* 8(4), e1002660 (2012), [doi:10.1371/journal.pgen.1002660](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1002660). On 31 October 2026 UTC I read the publisher's abstract, selected model and sampling passages, the figure 4 caption, the associated identifiability paragraph and the discussion of topology versus coalescence-time information. The same selected figure 4 passage was checked against the publisher's [10-page PDF text](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pgen.1002660&type=printable), printed page 8; those two representations are the same publication, not independent studies. The full paper, proof and figures were not independently reviewed, and no article bytes or page images were retained.

The paper works with probabilities of gene-tree **topologies** in a network coalescent, including multiple alleles per species, and studies particular simulation and inference settings. In its figure 4 example, the caption describes two hybridization probabilities and extinction. For that particular known network, the authors state that branch-length and hybridization parameters are not identifiable with the described single-allele sampling, and report identifiability when at least two alleles are sampled from species B. They also caution about the potentially very large number of gene trees needed in that example. The separately stated numerical experiment in the adjacent caption treats certain branch lengths as known; it must not be recast as simultaneous unconstrained estimation in every setting.

The paper points to its Text S1 for the detailed calculation and conditions. The publisher's supplementary link returned a server error after a storage redirect in the earlier session; the Europe PMC and PMC supplementary URLs also could not be opened through that web interface. On 1 November 2026 UTC, a [ResearchGate page listing the paper's three authors and a file named for Text S1](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/293983792_Text_S1) supplied extracted text headed with the same article title. I read selected §7 text on printed pages 17–18 and the caption of Figure S11. Its PDF download returned HTTP 403; I did not bypass that response, inspect the figure panels, or compare the hosted bytes with the publisher's supplement. The author-listed webpage is a source lead, not an authenticated byte-identical publisher copy or a second independent study.

In that text, the authors discuss a two-reticulation Scenario II with a single sampled allele from A and C and two from B. They group 15 gene-tree topologies into nine categories and contrast two particular parameter settings, reporting different topology probabilities at a specified short interval and a diminishing gap for longer intervals. I have not visually compared that scenario's diagram with Figure 4A of the main article. The passage also invokes the number of topology categories relative to parameters in discussing identifiability. **That count alone does not prove that a parameter-to-distribution map is one-to-one**, and distinguishing the two displayed settings does not by itself rule out every other collision. For example, the four strictly positive probabilities `(a/2, a/2, (1-a)/2, (1-a)/2)` sum to one for any `a` in `(0,1)` but do not identify a second parameter `b` in `(0,1)`, even though there are more outcomes than parameters. This is a general logical illustration, not a counterexample to the authors' model or a claim that their conclusion is false. I have not independently audited all their derivations or figures, and their full parameter-domain and sampling conditions remain to be resolved.

The reported two-reticulation/extinction example and topology frequencies differ from the present **one** directed pulse, fixed six labelled samples, known clock and within-locus two-site sequence law. The comparison does not transfer a sample-size claim or prove or disprove the nine-feature result.

## Zhu and Degnan (2017): gene-tree distributions and allele sampling

Sha Zhu and James H. Degnan, [“Displayed Trees Do Not Determine Distinguishability Under the Network Multispecies Coalescent”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27780899/), *Systematic Biology* 66(2), 283–298 (2017), [doi:10.1093/sysbio/syw097](https://doi.org/10.1093/sysbio/syw097). The PubMed connector returned the bibliographic record and authors' abstract on 31 October 2026 UTC. A search result supplied cached extracted passages from the [publisher's article page](https://academic.oup.com/sysbio/article/66/2/283/2682287), including its introduction and summary; direct opening of that page failed with a server error. The PMC page presented a browser check, and two attempts to retrieve rights-eligible full text through the PubMed connector returned an internal error. No article bytes or page images were retained, and the full proof and figures were not inspected.

The authors describe distributions of **gene-tree topologies** under a network multispecies coalescent with hybridization and incomplete lineage sorting. They give cases in which additional lineages from a species help distinguish networks that coincide for one sampled lineage per species, and a separate example where networks displaying the same trees can be distinguished even with one lineage per species. Their abstract presents a derived linear-combination representation of gene-tree distributions; it should not be paraphrased as a claim that every tree-mixture representation is invalid.

The cached summary explicitly says their examples do not establish that either network is distinguishable from every competing network, or that all numerical parameters are identifiable when a topology is known. Their observations and target differ from this manuscript's two homologous sequence columns, fixed directed three-species family and nine parameter values. This paper supports careful treatment of allele sampling and the difference between displayed trees and gene-tree distributions; it neither proves nor disproves the manuscript's exact nine-feature claim.

## Cummings and colleagues (2026): quintet concordance factors

Joseph Cummings, Maize Curiel, Bryan Currie, Bryson Kagy, Udani Ranasinghe and John A. Rhodes, [“Identifiability of phylogenetic networks and quintet concordance factors,” arXiv:2608.03544v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.03544v1), submitted 4 August 2026. On 31 October 2026 UTC I read the abstract, introduction, §§2.1–2.3, selected §4.3 statements and qualifications, and discussion in the arXiv HTML. I did not inspect the figures visually, run the accompanying Macaulay2 code, audit the proofs, or establish a later publication status.

The preprint studies distributions of unrooted **five-taxon gene-tree topologies**, called quintet concordance factors, under the network multispecies coalescent. Its main computational study restricts attention to rooted binary level-1 networks on five taxa without 2-cycles; the stated Theorem 4.7 is about algebraic identifiability for generic parameter values in a specified tree-of-blobs case. The paper uses coalescent-unit edge lengths and explains that pendant lengths can be omitted with one sample per taxon. This is a different observation and sampling setup from six labelled copies across three species under a known JC clock.

The authors caution that their computations do not supply sharp identifiability statements in all cases. In the discussion immediately before Theorem 4.8 they specifically describe a case where numerical evidence and incomplete low-degree invariant computations do not certify a definitive non-identifiability conclusion. That qualification should remain attached to that case; absence of a distinguishing invariant in the reported computation is not automatically equality of probability laws. The preprint does not establish the present manuscript's all-interior nine-parameter recovery, and this comparison does not transfer its network-topology results to the present source model.

## Currie and colleagues (2026): three-leaf triangles without coalescence

Bryan Currie, Aviva K. Englander, Jose A. Esparza-Lozano, Elizabeth Gross, Max Hill, Colby Long, Devon Olds, Kawika O'Connor, Udani Ranasinghe and Christin Sum, [“Semialgebraic Conditions for Identifying Triangles in Phylogenetic Networks,” arXiv:2606.26673v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26673v1), submitted 25 June 2026. On 1 November 2026 UTC I read the abstract, §§2.1, 2.2, 2.4 and 3.1 and selected discussion in the [v1 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26673v1). The arXiv submission history displayed v1 on that date; this does not establish journal publication or a comprehensive literature search. I did not inspect the figures visually, audit the full proofs or independently verify the paper's claim that its chosen Fourier coordinates contain all the one-site information.

Their model uses a Jukes–Cantor **single-site** pattern on a three-leaf network, with a displayed-tree mixture and explicitly **no coalescent process**. For one fixed triangle network, §2.4 writes the one-site law using six edge parameters and a mixing parameter and identifies four representative Fourier coordinates. Section 3.1 reports non-identifiability of the numerical parameters even within that fixed network. It gives a two-parameter transformation preserving the stated coordinates; after combining certain parameters, it also reports a generically one-dimensional fibre. The authors separately study intersections between differently oriented networks. That orientation question is not a claim that every pair of orientations is indistinguishable.

As a limited algebra check, substituting their equation (7) into equation (6) leaves the two combinations `δ a₁ a₄` and `(1−δ) a₁ a₆` unchanged and leaves `a₂`, `a₃` and `a₅` fixed. Consequently all four displayed representative coordinates stay fixed when the transformed parameters remain in the paper's domain. This checks the displayed substitution, not the proof that those coordinates determine the complete site-pattern law or the paper's other results.

The present manuscript instead fixes a directed pulse and backbone, samples six labelled copies from three taxa and uses a two-site law in which both sites share a random coalescent genealogy. It imposes its own strict time domain, rate ties and known clock. These differences do not isolate one cause for the different identifiability conclusions. The preprint's discussion mentions a zero-population-size limit for an ultrametric network model, but no mapping has been established here from that limit to the present two-site **joint** law or its parameter domain. A one-site equality alone does not establish equality of a two-site shared-genealogy law. Thus the preprint is relevant context, not a direct counterexample or independent verification of this manuscript's nine-feature claim.
